Why kitchen linen is judged in the wash, not on the shelf

Kitchen linen is the hardest working category we make. A cushion cover might be laundered a few times a year. A tea towel goes through the machine every week, sometimes more, and it is expected to come out square, bright and absorbent every single time. That is the frame I would ask any buyer to hold while reading this guide: you are not specifying how the product looks on day one, you are specifying how it behaves on wash fifty.

The category also punishes vague tech packs more than most. Tea towels, aprons, tablecloths, runners, placemats and napkins are simple constructions, so the price differences between suppliers come down to grams of fabric, centimetres of hem and the quality of small components like ties and loops. If those details are not written down, they drift. In this guide I go through each product in our Kitchen & Dining range the way we spec them with buyers, and finish with the checks worth running on any sample, whoever makes it. If you are comparing suppliers in this category, our kitchen linen manufacturing in India page covers capability in more depth.

Tea towels: weaves, weights and absorbency

A tea towel has one job, and a surprising number of them are bad at it. The construction decisions that decide performance:

  • Plain woven cotton is the standard. It prints beautifully, dries quickly on the rail and gives a flat surface for glassware. Most retail programmes sit between 150 and 220 GSM. Below that the cloth feels mean and saturates fast; well above it you are drifting towards a hand towel.
  • Waffle and honeycomb weaves trade the flat printable face for structure. The cells create surface area, so the cloth carries more water at the same weight and looks premium in solid colours. They are the natural partner for a plain printed towel in a two-pack or three-pack.
  • Yarn-dyed checks and stripes put the design into the weave itself. Butcher stripes and gingham checks never wash out because there is nothing on the surface to wash off. They cost more in loom set-up and repay it in longevity, the same logic that applies to woven designs in bed linen, which we cover in our bed linen fabric guide.
  • Linen and linen union glass cloths serve the traditional end of the market. Linen sheds almost no lint, which is why bartenders still polish glasses with it.

The standard retail size is 50×70 cm, with some markets preferring 45×65 cm or 50×76 cm. Specify the hem width, insist on a hanging loop if your market expects one, and decide where the loop sits: corner or centre, it should be consistent across the range.

Founder note The most common tea towel complaint has nothing to do with the weave. A brand new towel that smears water around instead of picking it up is carrying finishing residue from the mill, because the fabric was not properly desized and washed before making up. It corrects after a few domestic washes, but the customer has already formed an opinion by then. When we develop a tea towel programme, the absorbency check happens on the finished, washed towel, not on the loomstate fabric. Ask your supplier to show you the same thing: pour a little water on a table and wipe it with the sample. Ten seconds tells you everything.

Aprons: fabric, fit and the hardware that fails first

Aprons look like the simplest product on this page and generate the most returns per unit when they are under-specified. The cloth needs more body than a tea towel: we normally recommend a cotton canvas or drill between 180 and 280 GSM, depending on whether the range story is light and decorative or closer to workwear. A standard adult bib apron finishes around 70×90 cm, and a good tech pack states the finished dimensions at chest and hem, not just one width.

Then come the components, which is where cheap aprons quietly fail:

  • Neck strap: adjustable beats fixed. Sliding adjusters or a pair of D rings cost very little and remove the single biggest fit complaint.
  • Waist ties: long enough to wrap around and tie at the front on most body shapes. Short ties are the second biggest complaint.
  • Attachment points: every strap and tie should be bar tacked or box stitched. A tie that is only caught in the hem seam will pull out in the first months of use.
  • Pockets: a divided front pocket is standard. Bar tacks at the pocket corners again, because that is where the load goes.

Aprons also carry the print story of a kitchen range on the largest canvas, so print placement needs a diagram in the tech pack. A motif that sits perfectly on a flat sample can land in the wrong place once the neck is adjusted, which is why we approve apron strike-offs on a body form rather than a table.

Table linen: cloths, runners, placemats and napkins

Table linen is really a sizing exercise wrapped in a fabric decision. The fabric first: pure cotton dominates the mainstream, linen and linen cotton blends carry the premium and relaxed end, and heavier cottons with a stain release finish serve hospitality style programmes. Whatever the fibre, table linen wants a slightly heavier cloth than a tea towel so it drapes and sits flat rather than floating.

The sizes are driven by tables, not by fashion. A tablecloth should overhang the table by a drop of 20 to 30 cm on each side, which is how the common rectangles came to be: 140×180 cm for a four seater, 140×230 cm for six, 150×250 cm for eight. Napkins run 40×40 cm or 45×45 cm, runners around 33×180 cm, placemats around 33×45 cm. Build the range so the pieces set a table together: cloth or runner, placemats, napkins, all sharing bases and shades.

Two construction details separate professional table linen from the rest. First, double-fold hems with mitred corners. A mitred corner lies flat, launders flat and irons flat; an overlapped corner bulks up and creases badly. Second, shrinkage control. A tablecloth is the one product in this category where a few percent of shrinkage is immediately visible, because the drop gets shorter on every side at once. Agree the shrinkage tolerance in the tech pack and check it on a washed sample before bulk.

Prints, yarn-dyed checks and building a coordinated range

Kitchen linen ranges sell as families. The shopper who picks up a printed tea towel is being invited to add the matching apron, the runner and the napkins, so the design decisions should be made across the range rather than product by product. The techniques are the same ones we run for our cushion ranges on the same printing tables: screen printing for bold repeating designs, digital for detail and short runs, embroidery for accent pieces like runners and napkin sets. Yarn-dyed checks and stripes woven in-house sit alongside them, and our infrastructure page shows where each process happens.

The commercial structure that works is the same one I describe in every category: one or two base cloths, an edited palette of solids, one print story and one woven story stretched across every product in the range. That concentration is what keeps set-ups shared, minimums low and flexible, and repeats fast, for the reasons explained in our MOQ guide. A kitchen range built this way can put a dozen products on the shelf from a handful of fabric set-ups.

One more decision belongs at the start, not the end: certification. If the range will carry an organic or recycled claim, the fibre has to be certified from the beginning of development. Our explainer on OEKO-TEX, GOTS and GRS sets out what each label actually proves.

What to check on the sample before you approve bulk

Kitchen linen sampling is quick to judge if you know where to look. The checklist we encourage buyers to run:

  1. Fabric weight against the agreed GSM, on the finished product, not the greige quotation.
  2. Absorbency on tea towels: the wet table test described above.
  3. One domestic wash cycle, then measure again. Shrinkage within tolerance, colours holding, print still sharp, hems and mitred corners lying flat.
  4. Colour fastness, especially on deep shades and anything destined to meet food, steam and repeated hot washes.
  5. Components: loops, ties, adjusters and bar tacks on aprons; pull on them harder than a customer would.
  6. Labels and packing: care content, composition, origin, which for our production is made in India, and the folded presentation your shelf or peg expects.

This is a compressed version of the full quality chain that runs inside a factory, from fabric inspection through inline checks to the final AQL audit. If you want the complete picture, I wrote it up in how quality control actually works inside a home textile factory.

Frequently asked questions

What GSM should a tea towel be?

Most retail tea towels sit between 150 and 220 GSM in plain woven cotton. Lighter cloths around 150 to 180 GSM dry glassware well and cost less, while heavier cloths from 200 GSM upwards feel more substantial and hold more water before they saturate. Waffle and honeycomb weaves carry more water at the same weight because the structure creates extra surface area. Whatever you choose, write the weight into the tech pack and check it on the bulk fabric, because weight is the easiest place for a quotation to quietly lose a few grams.

What are the standard sizes for tea towels, aprons and table linen?

Tea towels commonly run 50×70 cm, with some markets preferring 45×65 cm or 50×76 cm. A standard adult bib apron is around 70×90 cm with an adjustable neck strap and waist ties long enough to bring back to the front. Tablecloths are built around table sizes plus a drop of 20 to 30 cm on each side, with 140×180 cm, 140×230 cm and 150×250 cm the common rectangles. Napkins are usually 40×40 cm or 45×45 cm, runners around 33×180 cm and placemats around 33×45 cm.

Which fabric is best for table linen, cotton or linen?

Pure cotton is the commercial standard. It prints and dyes well, launders predictably and hits mainstream price points. Pure linen and linen cotton blends carry a premium, relaxed look with natural slubs and gentle creasing, and they suit higher price bands. For hospitality style ranges, a heavier cotton with a stain release finish is worth considering. The honest answer is to decide the retail price first and let that choose the fibre, not the other way round.

What should a retailer check on a kitchen linen sample?

Wash it. Check shrinkage against the agreed tolerance, colour fastness after washing, whether a printed design still looks sharp, and whether hems and mitred corners stay flat. Test absorbency on tea towels by wiping a wet surface: a cloth that pushes water around instead of picking it up still has finishing residue in it. Check loops, ties and bar tacks on aprons, and measure everything again after the wash, because kitchen linen is judged over dozens of laundry cycles, not on day one.

Closing thought

Nothing in this category is technically difficult. That is exactly why specification discipline matters so much: when every factory can make a tea towel, the difference between a good programme and a disappointing one lives in the grams, the centimetres and the bar tacks that were or were not written down. Get the tech pack right and kitchen linen becomes one of the most reliable, repeatable categories a retailer can run.
