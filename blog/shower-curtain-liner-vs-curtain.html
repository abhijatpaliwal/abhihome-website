Shower curtain liner vs curtain: a buyer's guide

By Abhijat Paliwal, founder, Abhi Home®, third-generation Indian home textile manufacturer (since 1965).

Shoppers ask the same question in every bath aisle: do I need a liner, a curtain, or both? For a retailer or importer, the answer decides what goes into the range, how each piece is labelled and how many returns follow. I am Abhijat Paliwal, and at Abhi Home® we have made home textiles in India since 1965. We make cotton shower curtains as OEM / private label production under the buyer's brand, alongside cotton bath mats and bath rugs. This guide settles the shower curtain liner vs curtain question from a buyer's point of view: what sets the two apart, and how to plan them so they work together.

A curtain and a liner do two different jobs

The simplest way to explain the difference is by position. The liner hangs on the inside, closest to the water. Its job is to keep spray inside the bath or shower tray and to take the daily soaking. The curtain hangs on the outside, facing the room. Its job is to look good, give privacy and carry the design story of the bathroom.

Because the jobs differ, so do the products. A liner is usually thin, plain and replaced or washed often. A curtain is the largest piece of textile in most bathrooms, chosen for colour, pattern and drape, and expected to last.

Some products do both jobs at once, which is where the confusion starts. For a buyer, the useful question is not "curtain or liner?" but "which job does each piece in my range do?"

Materials: cotton cloth, polyester and plastic film

Most of the products in this category fall into three material groups, and each behaves differently in a wet room.

  • Plastic film. PVC (often called vinyl) and PEVA are the common liner films. They block water fully and are often sold with a weighted hem, but they crease in the pack and are usually treated as a replaceable item. PEVA is often chosen by buyers who want a liner without chlorine in the polymer.
  • Polyester fabric. Polyester liners and curtains are woven or knitted textiles, usually with a water resistant treatment. They wash in a machine and hang more softly than film.
  • Cotton cloth. This is the decorative end of the category and the construction we specialise in. Our cotton shower curtains are 100% cotton with a water repellent finish as standard, so water beads and runs off rather than soaking straight through. The design is tufted, woven or printed into the cloth, and the curtain goes in a domestic wash.

Within cotton, the surface construction decides the look. A tufted relief design raises a pattern in chenille yarn on the base cloth, so all the interest is in texture. Tone on tone white designs built this way suit premium and hospitality ranges, and nothing on the surface can fade or peel.

Print is the other main route. Screen print gives crisp colour and a hand drawn character, while digital print handles photographic and multi colour artwork with no screen cost. With a printed cotton curtain, the liner behind it matters more than buyers expect, because a dark or patterned liner can show through a light ground.

When a fabric curtain needs a liner

Whether a cotton curtain needs a liner depends on the bathroom, not on the curtain. In a walk in enclosure, or a well ventilated bathroom where the spray does not hit the panel hard, a fabric curtain with a water repellent finish is often enough on its own. It holds the spray back and dries between uses.

Over a bath, where water hits the curtain directly and at pressure, most customers prefer a liner behind it. The liner then also protects the decorative curtain.

Three ways to range curtains and liners

Retailers and importers usually choose one of three structures. Each has its own logic.

Range structureHow it worksBest suited to
Curtain onlyA fabric curtain with a water repellent finish, sold without a liner, with clear copy on where it works alone.Walk in showers, premium ranges, buyers who want a simple line.
Curtain plus separate linerThe decorative curtain is one item and a plain liner is listed as a separate accessory in matching sizes.Mixed customer bases, over bath showers, online ranges where the customer chooses.
Curtain and liner in one packBoth pieces are sold together as a set, with sizes and hanging holes matched.Gift and value ranges, hospitality packs, first time buyers.

The middle option is the most flexible. It keeps the decorative curtain as a long life product and treats the liner as the replaceable part. Our core programme is 100% cotton, not PVC, and where a buyer requires a liner we source it separately rather than substituting plastic for fabric.

Making the curtain and liner work as a pair

Most complaints come from the two pieces not fitting each other. Four details decide whether they hang as a pair.

  • Size. Our standard cotton curtain sizes are 180 x 180 cm and 180 x 200 cm, and in US imperial 72 x 72 in. and 72 x 84 in., with extra wide, extra long and stall sizes made to order. Specify the liner in the same width and drop, and decide whether the liner should finish slightly shorter so it sits inside the bath while the curtain hangs outside.
  • Hanging holes. Both pieces usually hang on the same hooks or rings. The holes must line up in number and spacing, or the panels pull against each other and the folds fall unevenly.
  • Header. Our standard is a reinforced buttonhole header with rust resistant eyelets, with tab top and grommet headers on request.
  • Colour of the liner. A clear liner shows the bathroom wall through a light curtain. A white or frosted liner behind a light cotton ground keeps the colour true. Check the pair together under bathroom lighting.

Write all four into the specification for the set. Our shower curtain buying guide for retailers covers sizes, headers and hems for the curtain itself in more depth.

Water repellent is not waterproof

The single most useful line of copy in this category is the one that separates water repellent from waterproof. A cotton curtain with a water repellent finish makes water bead and run off. It is not a waterproof membrane, and it should never be described as one. A plastic film liner, by contrast, blocks water.

Customers rarely mind this honesty; they mind discovering it after the floor gets wet. On the pack and listing, say the curtain is water repellent and machine washable, where it works alone and when a liner is recommended.

Finish claims need the same care. Anti microbial and flame retardant finishes are available to order on our curtains, the latter mainly for hospitality and contract work where a regulation or a client asks for it. Only claim a finish that is in the approved specification.

Labels and customs for each piece

A curtain and a liner can look like one product on the shelf, but for labelling and customs they are often two different things.

In the United States, the rules under the Textile Fiber Products Identification Act list "curtains and casements" and "draperies" among the products covered, according to 16 CFR 303.45 on the eCFR. A cotton or polyester fabric curtain is a textile product, so plan for fibre content, country of origin and the company name or registered number on the label. The same section excludes curtains made principally of slats, rods or strips of plastic and certain other materials.

The FTC guide to the Textile and Wool Acts explains that labelling is not required for non textile products or components, and that non fibrous parts such as plastic do not go on the fibre label. That means a plastic film liner and a fabric curtain may follow different labelling rules even when they are sold in one pack. A fabric liner, on the other hand, is a textile. Our guide to packaging and labelling for export covers what goes where on the label, hangtag and carton.

Customs works the same way. A cotton curtain and a plastic film liner are classified under different headings of the Harmonized Tariff Schedule, one among textile curtains and the other among plastic household articles. If you import a set, ask your customs broker how the combined pack and each component should be entered. For our production, the country of origin on the curtain is made in India.

What to test at sample stage

Hang the curtain and the liner together before you approve anything. The checklist we suggest:

  1. Fit. Do the holes line up? Do both panels hang straight, and does the liner finish where you want it inside the bath?
  2. Repellency. Spray water on the cotton curtain and watch it bead. Wash it once at the care label setting and repeat.
  3. Wash results. Measure for shrinkage after that wash, check for colour bleed and look for twist in the side hems.
  4. Show through. Look at the pair under bathroom lighting from outside the shower. Check that the liner does not change the colour of the curtain.
  5. Labels. Confirm that size, fibre content, care instructions and origin agree on the label, the hangtag, the carton and the listing.

For chemical safety, our cotton shower curtains are produced under OEKO-TEX STANDARD 100, with GOTS organic cotton and GRS recycled blends available on request. If you add a liner, ask the liner supplier for test reports that cover its own material and your market's requirements, because a certificate on the curtain does not cover a separate product. Our guide to OEKO-TEX, GOTS and GRS explains what each certificate proves, and our sampling guide sets out each approval stage from lab dip to pre production sample.

Building the bath range around the curtain

A shower curtain sold on its own is a single line. The same curtain with a matching bath mat becomes a set, and a set gives the customer a reason to buy more than one piece.

Coordinated bath sets usually fail on colour, when the curtain and the mat come from different factories and the whites do not match. Our shower curtains are matched to our cotton tufted bath mats and kids' shaped bath mats using shared dye lots, so the colours line up on the shelf. Stripe designs are a good place to start, because they repeat easily across a curtain, a bath rug and a runner.

If you are planning the floor side of the range as well, our guide to bath mat and bath rug formats, sets and runners covers how to build across sizes, and the bath textiles range shows the constructions we make.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a shower curtain and a shower curtain liner?

The liner hangs on the inside, closest to the water, and its job is to keep spray in the bath or shower tray. It is usually plain plastic film or a water resistant polyester fabric. The curtain hangs on the outside, facing the room, and its job is to look good, give privacy and carry the design.

Can a cotton shower curtain be used without a liner?

Often, yes. In a walk in enclosure or a well ventilated bathroom, a cotton curtain with a water repellent finish usually works on its own. Over a bath, where water hits the panel directly at pressure, most customers prefer a liner behind it. Cotton is water repellent, not waterproof, so say this plainly on the pack and in the listing.

Should the liner be the same size as the curtain?

Match the width and the hanging holes so both pieces hang from the same hooks without pulling. Many buyers specify the liner slightly shorter than the curtain so it sits inside the bath while the decorative curtain hangs outside. Check the pair hanging together at sample stage.

Does Abhi Home® make shower curtain liners?

Our core programme is 100% cotton shower curtains with a water repellent finish, not PVC. Where a buyer requires a liner, we source it separately rather than substituting plastic for fabric.
