Quilt and bedspread buying guide: fills, stitching and sizes

Why quilts get specified badly more often than sheets

Bed linen is easy to write a tech pack for. Name the fibre, construction, thread count and finished size, and most of the product is described. We covered that ground in our bed linen fabric guide. A quilt is a different animal. Three layers instead of one, stitching that holds those layers in a particular relationship, and a filling that can move. All three change after the first wash.

That is why quilts produce more surprises at sample stage than almost anything else we make. The fabric was approved. The print was approved. Then the quilt returns from a wash cycle two centimetres shorter on one side with the fill bunched into a corner, and nobody can say which line of the specification was wrong, because the line was never written. This guide follows the decisions in the order we take them with buyers developing a quilt and bedspread range, and names the specifications that prevent that.

Quilt, bedspread, coverlet, comforter: what the words mean

Agree on vocabulary first. Buyers and factories use these words differently across markets, and I have seen development cycles wasted on a misunderstanding a short conversation would have cleared.

  • Quilt. A construction, not a size. Three layers, stitched through. The stitching is the defining feature.
  • Bedspread. A size idea: a top cover cut large enough to hang down the sides of the bed and usually cover the pillows, quilted or flat.
  • Coverlet. Lighter and smaller than a bedspread, meant to sit over a duvet or a made bed as a decorative layer. Most of what retailers now buy from India falls here.
  • Comforter or duvet. A thick, heavily filled quilt used on its own for warmth, where fill weight drives the product rather than the face design.

Most international ranges are lightly filled coverlets or quilted bedspreads: in warm markets the only bed cover, in cold markets a layer over the duvet. That dual use is worth protecting when you set the fill weight.

Fills: cotton wadding, fibre fill, recycled fill and none at all

The fill decides how the quilt feels in the hand and how it photographs on a bed. Four routes:

  • Cotton wadding. The traditional fill, and still the right answer for a flat, dense, handcrafted look. It drapes rather than puffs and suits kantha and hand stitched styles where the point is the stitch, not the loft. Heavier than polyester for the same thickness, which matters for freight.
  • Polyester fibre fill. Loft without weight, at a sensible price, and what most volume programmes run on. Quality varies enormously, so specify it properly rather than asking for polyester and hoping.
  • Recycled polyester fill. Made from reclaimed bottle flake, behaves close to virgin fill, and lets the quilt carry a GRS claim if the whole chain is certified. Our guide to recycled and organic fibre options covers what that requires.
  • No fill. Plenty of handsome bedspreads have two layers and no wadding, relying on a heavier woven face for body. Lighter, cheaper to ship, worth considering for summer ranges.

Whichever route you take, the specification to write down is fill weight in grams per square metre, not a word like light or medium. Those words mean different things in different factories. A number means the same thing everywhere, and it fixes loft, drape, warmth and cost together.

The shell: face fabric, backing and why both matter

The face carries the design. On our floors that usually means a cotton base taking print, block print, patchwork or embroidery, the same techniques we use across cushions and throws. Printed cotton percale and voile dominate the lighter end, slub and yarn dyed cottons give texture at mid price points, and pieced patchwork and appliqué faces sit at the top, where the work is in hands rather than machines.

The backing is where buyers under specify most often, and where cost gets quietly cut. One lighter than the face will pull, pucker and wear through before the face does. My rule of thumb: comparable weight to the face, in a plain or coordinating shade, specified with the same care. Many best selling ranges use a reversible construction, two different prints front and back, which doubles the styling story at no extra cutting cost.

One more line worth adding: whether the face fabric is pre-washed before quilting. It costs a little and removes most of the shrinkage risk before the layers are joined. The cheapest insurance in this category.

Stitching: hand kantha, channel quilting and free motion

The stitching is not decoration, it is structure. It stops the fill migrating, and its spacing governs whether the quilt still looks right after twenty washes.

Hand kantha and hand quilting

Rows of running stitch worked by hand across the whole quilt. The surface has a gentle, irregular ripple no machine reproduces, and it is one of the things India does better than anywhere else. Slow to produce, priced by stitching density, and the reason many buyers come to us.

Channel and grid quilting

Straight parallel lines, or lines crossing into squares and diamonds, run on machine. Predictable, fast, the workhorse of volume ranges. Specify the spacing: lines far apart give a soft, puffy look but let the fill shift, while close lines hold it firmly and read flatter.

Free motion and panel quilting

Curves, florals and all over patterns stitched on a quilting machine. Useful when the quilting pattern itself is the design story. Panel quilting, where the stitch follows the printed design, looks exceptional when registration is right and wrong when it drifts, so judge it on a full size sample, not a swatch.

Founder note If I could persuade every buyer to write one extra line in a quilt tech pack, it would be the maximum stitch spacing. Not the pattern, the spacing. Nearly every complaint I have seen about a quilt losing shape traces back to fill with too much room to travel. Loft looks wonderful in the first product photograph and causes trouble by the third wash. Tighter stitching costs a little more, and it is the difference between a quilt that still looks like itself a year later and one that comes back.

Sizes, drop and building a range that makes sense

Bed names are unreliable across markets. A queen in one country is not a queen in another, so work from mattress dimensions rather than labels. The sizes we see most often are around 150×220 cm for single, 200×220 cm or 230×250 cm for double, 230×250 cm for queen and 260×280 cm for king, with pillow shams around 50×75 cm.

The number that actually drives the cut is the drop: how far the quilt hangs down each side of a made bed. A coverlet over a duvet needs very little. A traditional bedspread covering the base needs a lot. Two quilts for the same mattress can differ by thirty centimetres in cut width because of this alone, so settle the drop before anyone quotes.

On range architecture, the pattern that works commercially is the one we recommend across bedding generally. Hold to two or three sizes, run them across an edited group of designs, and keep base cloths and quilting set-ups concentrated. Add a sham or a matching cushion to lift basket value rather than a fourth size that fragments production. Sets photograph better than single pieces and cost less than most buyers assume, because the components share cloth.

Edges, washing and the finishing that decides returns

Edges take more abrasion than any other part of a quilt. Two approaches dominate, and they are not interchangeable:

  • Bias binding. A separate strip of fabric wrapped around the edge and stitched down. Strong, crisp, and easy to make a feature in a contrast shade. Specify the binding width and keep it even, since a wandering binding is the fastest way a good quilt looks homemade.
  • Turned and topstitched edge. The face and backing folded in on each other and stitched. Cleaner and softer, slightly less robust, and well suited to lighter coverlets.

Washing, in this category, is not a detail. Three layers shrink at different rates, so finished size and squareness after laundering are properties of the whole construction, not of the fabric alone. Agree a shrinkage tolerance, ask whether the quilt is garment washed after making up, and treat the care label as part of the specification. It carries fibre content, wash instructions and origin, which for our production is made in India.

Quilts are also bulky, so how they are folded and bagged decides shelf presentation and the freight bill. Our packaging and labelling guide covers what to specify.

What to check on the sample before you approve bulk

Judge a quilt sample on a bed, not a table, and wash it before signing anything. The checklist we encourage buyers to run:

  1. Fill distribution. Hold the quilt up against a window. Even light through the layers means even fill; bright and dense patches mean trouble.
  2. Stitch spacing and quality. Measure the spacing against the specification, look for skipped stitches, and check every line passes through all three layers.
  3. Loft against fill weight. Does the thickness match the grams per square metre you agreed? Substitution shows up here.
  4. Size and squareness after one wash. Measure all four sides before and after. Quilts reveal shrinkage and racking more than flat goods.
  5. Colour. Against the approved lab dip, in daylight and under store lighting. Our explainer on dyeing and printing covers why bulk shade never matches it exactly.
  6. Edges and backing. Even binding width, secure corners, and a backing that has not puckered.

None of this is exotic. It is the ordinary discipline of quality control in home textiles, applied to a product with more layers to go wrong. A manufacturer confident in its work welcomes scrutiny at sample stage, because every issue caught there never reaches a container. Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a quilt, a bedspread and a comforter?

A quilt has three layers: a face fabric, a layer of wadding and a backing, held together by stitching that runs through all three. A bedspread is the sizing idea rather than the construction. It is any top cover cut large enough to reach down the sides of the bed, and it may be quilted or completely flat. A comforter is a thicker quilt with a heavier fill, made to be used on its own for warmth. In practice most retail ranges from India are lightly filled quilts sold as bedspreads or coverlets, because that is what sells across warm and temperate markets alike.

Which fill is best for a retail quilt?

It depends on what the quilt is for. Cotton wadding gives a flat, dense, traditional drape and suits lightweight summer quilts and kantha styles. Polyester fibre fill gives loft and warmth for less weight and less money, which is why it dominates volume programmes. Recycled polyester fill behaves much like virgin fill and lets the range carry a GRS claim. Some bedspreads carry no fill at all and rely on a heavier woven or layered face instead. The specification that matters more than the fibre name is the fill weight in grams per square metre, because that is what decides loft, drape and the price.

What are the standard quilt and bedspread sizes for retail?

Common international retail sizes are roughly 150×220 cm for single, 200×220 cm or 230×250 cm for double, 230×250 cm for queen and 260×280 cm for king, usually paired with pillow shams around 50×75 cm. Markets vary, so always confirm against the mattress sizes your customers actually own rather than the label on the packaging. The important question is the drop, meaning how far the quilt hangs down the sides of a made bed, because that single number changes the cut size more than the bed name does.

What should a retailer check on a quilt sample before approving bulk?

Hold the quilt up to the light to see whether the fill is evenly distributed or has drifted into corners. Measure the stitch spacing against the specification and check that the stitching runs cleanly through all layers with no skipped sections. Confirm the finished size and squareness after a domestic wash, since shrinkage shows up most in quilted goods. Check the edge finish for an even binding width, look at colour against the approved lab dip, and feel the loft against the agreed fill weight. One wash cycle will tell you more about a quilt than any amount of inspection of it flat on a table.

Closing thought

Quilts reward attention to five numbers that are easy to leave out: fill weight, stitch spacing, drop, backing weight, shrinkage tolerance. Write them down and most of the risk in the category disappears. Leave them to the factory and you are trusting someone else's reading of your range.

This is a category where Indian making still has a real edge: hand quilting, block printing and patchwork are living skills here rather than revived ones. Bring us the look, the retail price band and the market, and we will say honestly what is achievable and where the cost sits. We run a structured sampling process so these decisions get tested before bulk, and we work with low and flexible MOQs so a first range need not be a leap.
