Throws and blankets: yarns, weaves and weights for retail ranges

Why this category gets underestimated

Throws look like the simplest thing we make. A rectangle of cloth, sometimes with a fringe, folded over the arm of a sofa. Buyers new to the category often treat it as an afterthought next to bedding or cushions, something to fill a corner of the range plan. Then the samples arrive and the first one feels thin and scratchy where the reference photo suggested soft and heavy, and the conversation changes fast.

The truth is that a throw carries almost no construction to hide behind. There is no insert, no lining, no zip to distract from a weak fabric. What you specify is exactly what the customer touches, so the yarn, the weave and the weight decisions in this guide matter more here than in almost any other throws and blankets category we produce. This piece walks through those decisions in the order we take them with buyers, ending with the checks worth running before bulk production starts.

Yarn choice: cotton, acrylic, wool and blends

Every throw programme starts with a yarn conversation, and the honest answer to "what's best" is always "best for what price and what season."

  • Cotton. Breathable, launders reliably, and takes colour well whether piece-dyed or yarn-dyed. It suits year-round ranges and lighter climates, and it is the easiest fibre to certify organic under GOTS if a range needs that story.
  • Acrylic. The volume workhorse of this category. Modern acrylic yarns get remarkably close to the softness of wool at a fraction of the price, and acrylic tolerates machine washing far better than wool does. Most mass-market sherpa and cable-knit throws are built on it.
  • Wool and wool blends. Genuine warmth and a premium hand feel that synthetic yarns still cannot fully copy, particularly in a merino or lambswool blend. The trade-off is cost and care: wool needs gentler washing, and that has to be stated clearly on the label.
  • Chenille. A yarn structure rather than a fibre, chenille can be spun from cotton, acrylic or blends. It gives a soft, slightly raised surface that photographs well and sits at a comfortable mid price point.
  • Recycled fibres. Recycled cotton and recycled polyester or acrylic both exist in throws now, usually under GRS certification. As with any recycled claim, the certificate has to follow the yarn from the spinner onward, not get added after the fact. Our explainer on what each certification actually proves is worth reading before you commit a range story to a fibre claim.

One detail that separates a good throw from a scratchy one has nothing to do with the fibre name at all. It is the yarn twist and the finishing wash. A softly spun, low-twist yarn that has been through a proper softening finish will out-handle a technically superior fibre that has been spun tight and finished cheaply. Always judge softness on the actual sample, not on the yarn's reputation.

Weave and knit constructions: waffle, herringbone, cable and sherpa

Construction is where a throw gets its personality. The main families we produce:

  • Waffle weave. A honeycomb texture woven directly into the cloth, giving surface interest and a slightly quilted look without any extra process. Popular in cotton because the texture also improves how the fabric drapes.
  • Herringbone and twill. Woven diagonal patterns that read as understated and tailored. These suit natural-fibre ranges where the texture should feel woven rather than decorated.
  • Cable knit. The classic chunky-knit look, made either on a knitting machine or, for smaller premium runs, hand knitted. Cable throws carry real bulk and warmth and photograph beautifully draped, but they are slower to produce than a woven equivalent.
  • Sherpa and faux fur. A knit or woven base fabric bonded or built with a plush pile face, sometimes on both sides. Extremely soft and warm, and currently one of the fastest-growing constructions in the category, especially in acrylic.
  • Jacquard. Complex patterns woven into the structure itself rather than printed on. Higher set-up cost, but the pattern never fades or washes off, which matters for a design-led range.

Every one of these constructions can be made well or made carelessly, and the difference rarely shows in a flat photograph. It shows when the throw is picked up.

Founder note A buyer once sent me a competitor's throw and asked why ours cost fifteen percent more for what looked like the same weave. I put both on a table, folded them the same way, and asked her to pick one up with her eyes closed. She chose ours without hesitating. The difference was not visible in a photograph. It was the yarn twist, the finishing wash and the density of the weave, three things a spec sheet can capture but a picture cannot. That is why I always tell buyers to handle a physical sample before they commit to a range, even when the timeline is tight.

Weight and drape: what GSM actually tells you

Weight is specified in GSM, the same measure we use across bed linen fabrics, and it tells a buyer two things at once: how the throw will feel and how it will drape. Lightweight woven throws for warmer climates typically run between roughly 250 and 400 GSM. Mid-weight woven and knitted pieces sit between roughly 400 and 650 GSM, and heavy sherpa or cable constructions climb from about 650 GSM upward, sometimes well past 1000 GSM for the plushest pieces.

Heavier is not automatically better. A lightweight cotton throw with the right drape will fall in soft folds over a sofa arm, while a stiff, over-finished heavy throw can sit awkwardly like a board. Drape depends on the weave structure and the finishing as much as the raw weight. When you develop a sample, ask to see it actually draped over furniture, not laid flat on a table, because that is the only honest test of how it will look in a customer's home.

Fringe, hems and the finishing details buyers overlook

The edge of a throw does a surprising amount of work. A hand-knotted fringe reads as artisanal and adds real production time, since each knot is typically tied by hand. A machine-whipped fringe is faster and more consistent but reads as more commercial. A simple folded and stitched hem is the quickest and cheapest option and suits minimal, tailored range stories.

Whichever finish you choose, specify the fringe length, the knot type if hand-tied, and how the edge is expected to behave after washing. Fringes are the first place a throw starts to look tired, through fraying, uneven knots working loose, or tangling in the wash. A well finished fringe should survive several domestic wash cycles without needing to be untangled by hand every time, and that is worth testing on the sample rather than assuming.

Sizes for sofa throws, bed throws and true blankets

Sizing splits cleanly by use. A sofa throw usually runs around 125×150 cm, generous enough to drape over an arm or the back of a cushion without looking mean. A bed throw, used decoratively across the foot of the bed or as a layer over the spread, is larger, often close to 220×240 cm. A true blanket, sized to actually cover a sleeping person for warmth, typically starts around 150×200 cm for a single and 200×230 cm for a double, and tends to favour plainer constructions since it is judged on warmth and washability rather than how it photographs folded.

The mistake I see most often is a single size doing double duty. A throw cut for a sofa is too small to look right across a bed, and a throw cut for a bed is oversized and awkward for a chair. If your range needs to serve both uses, plan two sizes on the same base cloth rather than compromising on one.

Building a range that shares set-ups

The commercial pattern that keeps this category profitable is the same one that works across our other product groups. Choose one or two base yarns and constructions, run them through an edited palette of solid shades and a jacquard or woven pattern story, and hold sizing to two or three options. That structure gives visual variety on the shelf while dye lots and knitting or loom set-ups stay concentrated, which is exactly how minimums stay low and flexible, as we cover in our guide to how MOQs really work.

Throws also coordinate naturally with other categories on the same shelf. A cable-knit throw in a shade that matches a cushion range, or a woven jacquard that echoes a pattern used in bedding, sells as a considered story rather than a random add-on. Because we knit, weave and finish across categories in one facility, described on our infrastructure page, coordinating yarns and shades across a range is straightforward to plan rather than something bolted on afterward.

What to check on the sample before you approve bulk

A throw sample rewards a slower, more physical inspection than most products in the catalogue. The checklist we walk buyers through:

  1. Handle: pick it up, do not just look at it. Softness, weight in the hand and how it falls when draped are the three things a photograph cannot show.
  2. Weight: against the agreed GSM, checked before and after a wash, since some constructions shrink or bulk up.
  3. Construction: stitch or knit density even across the piece, no thin patches or dropped stitches, and pattern registration correct on any jacquard or woven design.
  4. Edges and fringe: knot security, fringe length consistency, and hem stitching straight and even along the full length.
  5. Colour: against the approved lab dip in both daylight and store lighting, and no bleed when rubbed damp against a white cloth.
  6. After a wash: shrinkage against tolerance, pilling on knit constructions, and whether the drape and softness survive the cycle intact.

This is the same discipline we apply everywhere in the factory, laid out stage by stage in how quality control actually works inside a home textile factory. A throw that passes this checklist rarely comes back as a complaint.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a throw and a blanket?

A throw is styled first and used second. It is sized to drape over the arm of a sofa or the foot of a bed, and its job is mostly decorative, with warmth as a welcome side effect. A blanket is sized to actually cover a sleeping person, so it runs larger, is usually plainer, and is judged on warmth and washability rather than how it photographs folded on a chair. The two categories share yarns and constructions, but a range built for one rarely works unchanged for the other.

What yarn is best for a throw?

There is no single best yarn, only the right one for the price point and the story. Cotton is breathable, launders well and suits year-round ranges. Acrylic mimics wool's softness at a lower price and higher wash tolerance, which makes it the workhorse of volume programmes. Wool and wool blends carry genuine warmth and a premium hand feel but need more careful care instructions. Chenille and cotton-acrylic blends sit in between, adding softness without the full cost of wool. The decision usually comes down to retail price band and season.

What GSM should a throw be?

As a working range, lightweight woven throws for warmer climates typically sit between roughly 250 and 400 GSM, mid-weight woven and knitted throws between roughly 400 and 650 GSM, and heavy sherpa or cable knit pieces from about 650 GSM upward. These are orientation figures rather than fixed rules, and the right number depends on the drape you want and the season the range targets. What matters is that the weight is written into the tech pack, because a throw that looks identical in a photograph can feel completely different once it is in a customer's hands.

What size should a sofa throw be compared with a bed throw?

A sofa throw usually runs around 125 by 150 cm, generous enough to drape over an arm or a back cushion without looking skimpy. A bed throw is larger, often close to 220 by 240 cm, so it can sit across the foot of the bed or cover it fully as a decorative layer. A true single-bed blanket for warmth typically starts around 150 by 200 cm and a double closer to 200 by 230 cm. Ranges that blur these sizes usually end up with a throw too small to style well or a blanket too small to actually warm anyone.

Closing thought

Throws and blankets punish shortcuts more visibly than almost anything else we make, because there is nowhere for a weak specification to hide. Get the yarn, the construction and the weight right, finish the edges properly, and size each piece for the job it is actually meant to do, and the category becomes one of the easiest in the range to sell on repeat. Get any one of those wrong, and no amount of styling in the photograph will save it.
