Handcrafted pouffes and floor cushions: constructions and fills

A soft product that has to behave like furniture

Pouffes sit in an awkward gap. They are made on textile machinery, priced like textiles and bought by textile buyers, but the customer treats them as furniture. Someone will sit on it. A child will stand on it. It will be dragged across a floor by one corner. Very little else in a home textile range gets that kind of handling.

That gap is where the disappointments come from. A cushion that goes slightly soft is a minor annoyance. A pouffe that goes soft stops being a seat, and the customer notices within weeks. So while the design conversation is always about pattern and texture, the conversation that decides whether the product succeeds is about what is inside it and how the panels are joined. This guide covers both, in the order we take them with buyers developing a pouffe and floor seating range with us.

Constructions: woven, tufted, knitted and patchwork

The outer shell of a pouffe is where the design lives. Four families cover almost everything we make, and each carries a different cost logic.

  • Hand-woven panels. The pattern is in the cloth itself: ikat, chevrons, stripe bands, space-dyed yarns that give a marbled effect. Woven designs read as premium from any angle, hold their pattern permanently, and hide wear well because the colour goes through the yarn rather than sitting on the surface. Loom set-up costs sit at the front of the job, so woven designs reward being run in a proper colourway family rather than as a one-off.
  • Hand-tufted panels. Loop or cut pile worked into a ground cloth, giving raised geometric motifs, diamonds, dots and medallions. This is the most tactile construction and it photographs beautifully, which matters in a category sold heavily online. Pile anchorage is the thing to specify, because a tufted face that sheds becomes a returns problem.
  • Hand-knitted and crochet. Chunky yarn worked into a shell, usually over a soft fill. Distinctive, comfortable and slow to make. It suits ranges with a craft or Scandinavian story, and it prices accordingly.
  • Patchwork and colour-block. Panels of different weaves and shades assembled into one form, often with fringe or rib detail at the edges. Commercially useful, because it absorbs shorter fabric runs into a saleable design instead of leaving them idle.

Embroidery, appliqué and leather tab or handle details layer on top of any of these. A leather handle is worth calling out separately: it is the single detail buyers most often forget to specify, and it changes how the product is used, because a pouffe with a handle gets moved around the room rather than staying put.

Shapes, sizes and the heights that actually work

Two shapes dominate. The cube is the workhorse, structured and stackable in a display, and it usually sits around 40 cm on each side, with 45 cm used where a larger seat is wanted. The round or drum shape is softer and more casual, generally 50 to 55 cm across and 35 to 40 cm high.

Height is the specification that gets underestimated. Below roughly 35 cm a pouffe reads as a footstool and nobody sits on it. Above about 45 cm it starts to look precarious, and because these products have no rigid frame, tall shapes lean when weight goes on one edge. The comfortable seat window is narrow, which is why so many pouffe ranges converge on similar dimensions.

Floor cushions are a different product with a different job. They are for sitting on the floor, not for sitting at furniture height. Square floor cushions typically run 60×60 cm or 70×70 cm, round ones around 60 cm across, and both are usually 12 to 15 cm deep. They are closer in construction to an oversized cushion than to a pouffe, but they take floor abrasion, so the base fabric still has to be specified properly.

Fills: what goes inside and how each one behaves

This is the decision buyers spend the least time on and regret the most. The options, and what each one actually does over a year of use:

  • Shredded foam. The most common seating fill. Firm, resilient, recovers its shape after weight comes off, and it can be packed to a specified density so repeat orders feel the same as the first. It settles a little in the first weeks and then stabilises.
  • Moulded foam core with a fibre wrap. The most furniture like result. Crisp edges, a flat seating surface, no slump. It costs more and it ships as a rigid block, which affects packing. Right for premium lines where the pouffe is genuinely sold as seating.
  • Polyester fibre. Light, soft and inexpensive, and it compresses steadily under repeated load. Well suited to decorative floor cushions and to shapes that are meant to look relaxed. Not the fill for something a person sits on every evening.
  • Recycled textile offcuts. Dense, heavy and grounded, and it puts factory waste to use rather than sending it out. The weight is a genuine trade off: a pouffe filled this way stays where it is put, which some buyers want and others do not. If you intend to make a recycled claim on the label, get the content documented at the start rather than after development.
  • Expanded polystyrene beads. Very light, mould to the body, and popular in bean bag style seating. They settle noticeably with use, so the product needs a top-up opening and the customer needs to be told that in the care information.

Whichever fill you choose, put a weight or density figure in the tech pack rather than a word like "firm". Firm is a matter of opinion. A stated fill weight is something a factory can hit consistently and an inspector can verify. The same principle applies to cushion inserts, and we go into it further in our cushion filling guide.

Founder note The complaint I hear most about pouffes bought elsewhere is never about the fabric. It is always the same sentence: it went flat. Nine times out of ten that is an under-filled shell with a soft fibre fill, made to hit a price. A shell can be beautiful and the product will still fail if there is not enough of the right material inside it. When we quote a pouffe we quote the fill weight alongside the fabric, because those two numbers together are the product. Change one without the other and you have quoted a different thing.

Filled or unfilled: the freight arithmetic

A filled pouffe is mostly air, and air is expensive to ship. An unfilled cover packs almost flat, so a container carries several times more pieces and freight per unit drops sharply. That saving is real, and for larger shapes it can be the difference between a workable landed cost and an impossible one.

The cost of that saving is that somebody has to fill the covers near your market. That means a filling partner, a material cost, a labour step and storage for bulky finished goods before they reach the shelf. Retailers with a distribution centre and a filling arrangement usually come out ahead. Retailers shipping direct to store generally do not, because there is no point in the chain where filling can happen cheaply.

Filled goods arrive ready to sell and photograph correctly on day one, with no risk that a third party fills them to the wrong weight. The honest way to settle this is to have your manufacturer cost both routes to your destination port and compare landed cost per unit on the shelf, not per carton.

Base panels, seams and the parts that fail first

Everything that goes wrong with a pouffe goes wrong in one of three places.

The base panel. It never appears in the product photograph and it takes all the abrasion. It should be a stable, tightly woven fabric, not a lighter weight version of the decorative face. On floor cushions the same applies with more force, because the whole underside is in contact with the floor.

The seams. A pouffe seam is under constant outward pressure from the fill, and it takes a sharp load every time someone sits down. Corner and edge seams should be double stitched, or bound, rather than plain overlocked. Stitch density belongs in the tech pack. A seam that opens is not a repair, it is a return.

The closure. Whether the shell is filled in India or elsewhere, the opening has to survive being filled, and possibly refilled. A light dress-weight zip will not do it. Specify a heavy zip or a properly reinforced hand-closed opening, and check how the tape is anchored into the seam rather than just how the slider feels.

Handles deserve one line of their own: they must be anchored through the structural seam or through a reinforcement patch, not stitched to the face fabric alone. A handle that tears out takes a piece of the pouffe with it.

Building a range that shares its bases

The commercial logic here is the same as it is in cushions. Pick a small number of base constructions, then stretch them across colourways and sizes rather than inventing a new construction for every design. One woven quality in four colourways, one tufted quality in three, a patchwork design that consumes the short runs from both, and two shapes across the whole range. That structure fills a display wall while the loom set-ups and dye lots stay concentrated behind it, which is what keeps minimums low and repeat orders quick. Our MOQ guide explains why set-ups, not order size, are what really drive minimums.

Pouffes also merchandise well alongside adjacent categories. A pouffe in the same yarn family as a rug, or a woven quality echoed in a throw, gives a coordinated story on the shop floor without extra development work. Buyers who plan that crossover early get it almost free.

One practical note on packing: pouffes are bulky and awkward, and their packaging affects both freight and how they arrive. Compression packing, carton sizing and shelf presentation are worth settling early, and our guide to packaging and labelling for export covers what to put in the specification.

What to check on the sample

Judge a pouffe sample the way a customer will, which means sitting on it rather than looking at it on a table.

  1. Sit on it. Does it hold you comfortably, and does the top recover when you stand, or does it keep the dent?
  2. Fill weight. Check the actual weight against the agreed figure. This is the specification most likely to drift in bulk.
  3. Seams and base. Turn it over. Look at the base fabric, the seam construction and the stitch density, in that order.
  4. Surface. Pile anchorage on tufted panels, yarn tension on woven ones, and how the pattern meets at the corners where panels join.
  5. Colour. Against the approved lab dip, in daylight and under store lighting.
  6. Hardware. Zip weight and anchorage, handle or tab attachment, and how each is reinforced behind the face fabric.
  7. Time. Leave the sample filled and in use for a week or two, then look again. Settling and slump reveal themselves with time, never on the first day.

Any manufacturer who is confident in the work will welcome this. Every problem caught at sample stage is a problem that never reaches a container, which is the whole argument for the in-house making-up and inspection capacity we have built.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best filling for a pouffe?

It depends on whether the pouffe is meant to be sat on or looked at. Shredded foam gives firm, resilient support and is the most common choice for a pouffe used as a seat. A moulded foam core wrapped in fibre gives the crispest edges and the most furniture like feel, at a higher cost. Polyester fibre is soft and light but compresses over time, so it suits decorative floor cushions more than seating. Recycled textile offcuts are heavy, dense and low cost, and they work well where a solid, grounded feel is wanted. Expanded polystyrene beads are light and mouldable but they settle, so they need topping up.

Should I import pouffes filled or unfilled?

Unfilled covers ship far more efficiently because they pack almost flat, so a container carries several times more units and freight per piece drops sharply. The trade off is that someone has to fill them near your market, which means a second supplier, a filling cost and a labour step before the product reaches the shelf. Filled pouffes arrive ready to sell and photograph correctly on arrival, but you are shipping a great deal of air. Ask your manufacturer to cost both routes to your destination port and compare landed cost per unit on the shelf, because the answer changes by market and by product size.

What sizes are standard for pouffes and floor cushions?

Cube pouffes commonly sit around 40 cm on each side, with 45 cm used for a larger seat. Round or drum pouffes are usually 50 to 55 cm across and 35 to 40 cm high, which places the top at a comfortable footstool height. Square floor cushions are typically 60×60 cm or 70×70 cm, and round floor cushions around 60 cm across with a depth of 12 to 15 cm. Height matters more than width for a seat, because a pouffe that is too low reads as a footstool and one that is too tall looks unstable.

What should I check on a pouffe sample before approving bulk?

Sit on it, and then look at the seams. Check that the corner and edge seams are double stitched or bound rather than plain overlocked, that the base panel is a hard wearing fabric with a stable weave, and that the zip or closure is heavy enough for repeated filling. Press the top and see whether it recovers or holds a dent. Check pile anchorage on tufted panels, colour against the approved lab dip, and any handle or leather tab for how it is anchored through the outer fabric. Finally, leave the sample filled and loaded for a few days and look at it again, because settling shows up with time rather than on day one.

Closing thought

Pouffes and floor cushions are one of the most enjoyable categories we make, because they are where hand skills and structural thinking meet in the same product. The weaving, tufting and knitting are craft work that machines cannot replicate at this quality. The seams, the base panel and the fill weight are engineering decisions with right and wrong answers.

Get the craft right and the product sells itself on the shelf. Get the structure right and it is still doing its job three years later, which is what brings the customer back to the retailer. Both halves have to be specified, and specifying only the pretty half is the mistake that quietly costs the most.
