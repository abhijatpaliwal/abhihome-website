Shower curtain buying guide for retailers: fabrics, sizes and headers

The quietest problem in the bath aisle

Most buyers I meet have a clear point of view on bath mats. They know the constructions and the weights, and they have an opinion on backings. Ask the same buyer about shower curtains and the answer is vaguer. The curtain gets treated as the accessory that fills out the bath range, bought on price, replaced when the supplier changes.

That is a missed opportunity. The shower curtain is the largest piece of textile in a bathroom and the first thing a customer sees when they open the door. It is also where the gap between a cheap version and a good one is most obvious to the touch.

This guide walks through the decisions in the order we take them with buyers developing a shower curtain programme: cloth, construction, size, header, finish, and how the curtain earns its place in the range.

Cotton cloth or printed film: the decision that sets everything else

Almost every shower curtain on a mass market shelf is printed PVC or PEVA film. Cheap, waterproof, and it photographs acceptably online. It also creases in the pack, cracks at the fold lines, carries a smell when new, and cannot be laundered. That is why the category has such a short repurchase cycle.

A cotton shower curtain behaves like a textile. The pattern is tufted or woven into the cloth rather than printed onto a membrane, so there is nothing to peel, and it goes in a domestic wash. It hangs with weight and drape rather than clinging, and because it reads as an upgrade the moment someone touches it, it supports a price point that film never will.

The trade-off belongs in your own copy: cotton is water repellent, not waterproof. More on that below. The base cloth choices:

  • Pure cotton, plain woven. The default. Takes dye and print cleanly, launders predictably, and gives the panel enough body to hang straight. Specify the weight in GSM, because two curtains at the same price can drape very differently.
  • Yarn-dyed cotton. Colour dyed into the yarn before weaving, so stripes and checks run right through the cloth. It holds up through repeated washing, which matters in a wet room product.
  • Organic and recycled options. GOTS organic cotton and GRS recycled blends are available here as in any other category. Decide before fabric development, because the certification has to follow the fibre from the yarn stage. Our explainer on what each certification actually proves sets out the difference.

Four constructions, and what each one is good for

Once the cloth is settled, the surface decides the design story. Four techniques carry the category, each with its own cost logic:

  • Tufted relief. Chenille yarn tufted into the base cloth to raise a pattern. The strongest construction for premium white and tone-on-tone programmes, because all the interest is in texture. It photographs as depth rather than a flat graphic, and there is no printed layer to fade.
  • Yarn-dyed woven. Stripe, check and crosshatch woven from pre-dyed yarn. Costs more in loom set-up and nothing in printing, and wears its design permanently. A dense crosshatch also hides water marks well in daily use, which customers notice without being able to name it.
  • Screen print. Right for bold designs with a defined palette and the hand-drawn, artisanal look. Check registration where colours meet, and penetration, meaning whether the colour has gone into the cloth or only sat on the surface.
  • Digital print. Photographic and multi-colour artwork with no screen cost, which is what makes a short seasonal capsule viable in this category at all. Useful for testing a design story before committing to it.

Mixing two techniques in one panel is where in-house capability matters. A tufted stripe next to a printed motif has to be registered on the same table. When tufting and printing happen in two different factories, the elements drift out of alignment and the panel never looks right. Our manufacturing set-up keeps all four under one roof. Colour and print mechanics are covered in our guide to dyeing and printing home textiles.

Founder note The mistake I see most often is a curtain designed as a flat rectangle of artwork. A shower curtain is almost never seen flat. It is seen hanging, half drawn, with folds running down it. Artwork with a strong centre motif disappears into those folds. An even all-over repeat, a banded layout at head and hem, or raised texture survives the fold and still reads from the bathroom door. Ask to see the sample hanging and gathered before you sign it off, not pinned to a board.

Sizes, drop and the detail that drives returns

Sizing is more standardised than most buyers expect, and getting it wrong is expensive because the product becomes unusable rather than merely disappointing. The two metric sizes that cover most of the international market are 180×180 cm and 180×200 cm. In US imperial, 72×72 in. and 72×84 in.

The longer drop exists because a shower over a bath needs the curtain to reach inside the tub, while a walk-in enclosure needs length to the floor. Extra-wide, extra-long and stall sizes are made to order.

Two practical points. Write the size on the label in the unit your market reads, and check that the care label, the hangtag and the carton marking agree. A curtain marked in centimetres and listed online in inches generates avoidable returns. And specify your tolerance and whether the size is quoted before or after the first wash. Cotton moves, and a supplier who quotes an exact figure with no tolerance is telling you something.

Headers, eyelets and how the curtain actually hangs

The header is the part nobody specifies and everybody complains about. It carries the weight of a wet panel, every day, on a dozen small points. Get it wrong and the curtain tears at the holes within months.

  • Reinforced buttonhole header with eyelets. The retail standard and our default. The top band is reinforced so the load spreads across the header rather than pulling at each hole, and the eyelets are rust resistant, which matters more here than in any other curtain product because the environment is permanently humid.
  • Tab top. Fabric loops instead of hardware. No metal to corrode, a softer look, suited to natural and coastal range stories. The loops must be stitched into the header seam, not simply topstitched on.
  • Grommet header. Larger metal rings, a heavier contemporary look, and a smooth glide. Specify the finish and ask what the ring is made of, not just what colour it is plated.

Whichever you choose, the specification that matters is eyelet spacing and count, because that decides whether the folds fall evenly. Hems matter too. Side hems should be deep enough to stop the panel twisting, and a weightier bottom hem helps it hang straight instead of billowing inward. These are the same questions we work through on the window curtain range, in a much wetter environment.

Finishes, liners and honest copy on the hangtag

A water repellent finish is standard on our cotton curtains. It makes water bead and run off rather than soak through, and it is what lets a fabric curtain do the job at all. It is not a waterproof membrane and should never be sold as one.

That distinction is worth one clear line on your packaging. Say the curtain is water repellent and machine washable, and whether a liner is recommended. Customers do not mind the honesty. They mind discovering it themselves. In a walk-in enclosure the fabric curtain alone is usually enough. Over a bath, where water hits the panel at pressure, most customers prefer a liner, and the simplest answer is to list one as a separate accessory.

Anti-microbial and flame retardant finishes are available to order, the latter mainly for hospitality and contract work where regulation requires it. Whatever you specify, ask for the wash durability and test it at sample stage. A finish that disappears after three washes is a complaint waiting to happen.

Coordinating the curtain with the rest of the bath range

A shower curtain sold alone is a low-value line. The same curtain with a matching bath mat is a set, and a set lifts basket value without adding a new design. That is the argument for developing the two together.

There is a production argument too. Coordinated bath sets usually fail on colour. The curtain comes from one factory and the mat from another, the whites are different whites, and the two look wrong side by side on the shelf. When both come from the same yarn dye lots, they actually match. Our guide to bath mat constructions covers the mat decisions that sit alongside these.

The same logic extends outward. A tufted texture developed for a curtain can run as a bedspread or quilt surface, and a woven crosshatch into flatweave floor work. Reusing a design across categories keeps minimums sensible, as we explain in our MOQ guide.

What to check on the sample before you approve bulk

Hang the sample. A shower curtain only shows its faults under its own weight, and judging one flat on a table tells you nothing. The checklist we encourage buyers to run:

  1. Hang and drape: does the panel fall straight, hem level, no bowing in the middle?
  2. Header: reinforcement present, eyelets evenly spaced and set square, hardware rust resistant.
  3. Colour: against the signed-off lab dip, in daylight and under store lighting. The sampling guide explains what each approval stage does.
  4. Surface: tuft anchorage on tufted designs, registration and penetration on prints, weave density on yarn-dyed cloth.
  5. Finish: spray water on the panel and watch whether it beads. Wash it once and repeat the test.
  6. After the wash: measure for shrinkage, check for colour bleed, look for twist in the side hems.
  7. Labels and packing: size marking, care instructions, fibre content and country of origin, which for our production is made in India. Our guide to packaging and labelling for export covers what goes where.

Every fault caught here never reaches a container, and a manufacturer confident in its own work welcomes the scrutiny.

Frequently asked questions

What is the standard shower curtain size?

The two sizes that cover most of the international market are 180 x 180 cm and 180 x 200 cm in metric, and 72 x 72 in. and 72 x 84 in. in US imperial. The wider and longer variants exist because showers over baths and walk-in enclosures need more drop and more width to seal properly. Extra-wide, extra-long and stall sizes are made to order. If you sell into more than one market, decide early whether you are listing the size in centimetres, inches or both, because the care label, the hangtag and the carton marking all have to agree.

Are cotton shower curtains waterproof?

No, and it is important to say so on the packaging. A cotton shower curtain carries a water repellent finish, which means water beads and runs off rather than soaking straight through, but the cloth is not a waterproof membrane. In practice that is how a fabric curtain is meant to work: it holds the spray back, dries out between uses, and goes in the wash when it needs to. Buyers who need a true waterproof barrier pair the fabric curtain with a liner.

Do cotton shower curtains need a liner?

It depends on the bathroom, not on the curtain. In a walk-in enclosure or a well ventilated bathroom the fabric curtain alone is usually enough. Over a bath, where water hits the curtain directly at pressure, most customers prefer a liner behind it. Many retailers simply list a liner as a separate accessory and say plainly on the hangtag that the curtain is water repellent rather than waterproof. That one line of honest copy prevents a surprising number of returns.

What should a retailer check on a shower curtain sample?

Hang it before you judge it, because a shower curtain only shows its faults under its own weight. Check that the panel hangs straight with no bowing at the hem, that the header is reinforced and the eyelets are set evenly and are rust resistant, that the colour matches the approved lab dip, and that the water repellent finish still beads water after a domestic wash. Measure the panel before and after that wash for shrinkage, check side hems for twist, and confirm the care label, size marking and packing match what your listing will say.

Closing thought

The shower curtain is an easy product to buy badly and a satisfying one to buy well. Nothing here is complicated. Choose cloth over film, put the pattern into the cloth rather than onto it, specify the header properly, be honest about water repellency, and develop the curtain alongside the mat so the colours match on the shelf.

Do that and a line most retailers treat as filler becomes one customers notice and come back for. Tell us the sizes and price point you are working to and we will show you what is possible from the cloth up.
