Packaging and labelling home textiles for export: what buyers must specify

Why packaging is part of the product, not an afterthought

In most development conversations, packaging comes up last. The fabric has been approved, the samples have been signed off, the order is nearly placed, and then someone asks how the goods should actually arrive. I understand why it happens. Packaging is not glamorous. But I have seen more shipments delayed by missing label artwork than by anything that happens on a loom.

Here is the part that surprises first-time importers: your manufacturer cannot decide most of this for you. The care label content, the barcode numbers, the brand artwork, the carton assortments, all of it belongs to your side of the table, because the legal responsibility for what a label says sits with whoever places the product on sale in their market. What a good manufacturer can do is tell you exactly what to specify, flag what your destination market requires, and execute it precisely. That is what this guide covers, whether you are packing a bedding programme, a cushion range or a set of kitchen linens.

The care label: fibre content, symbols and origin

The sewn-in care label is the one piece of packaging that stays with the product for its whole life, and it is the one regulators actually check. Three things belong on it everywhere in the world:

  • Fibre composition. Stated by percentage, using the legal fibre names of the destination market, not trade names. "100% cotton" is fine. A brand name for a polyester blend is not.
  • Care instructions. Usually the wash symbols, in the sequence wash, bleach, dry, iron, professional care. Many buyers add short text instructions as well, and some markets expect text in the local language.
  • Country of origin. For our production this reads made in India. It must be truthful and legible, and in some markets it must be present at the point of sale, not only on the sewn label.

Beyond those three, requirements vary. Some markets want the importer's name and address on the label or the packaging. The United States uses registered identification numbers in place of a written company name if the brand prefers. Filled products such as cushions and quilts often need a separate law label or filling declaration stating what is inside. Your compliance team or import agent should confirm the exact list for each market; your manufacturer should then build the label to match, position it consistently, and never substitute stock labels because artwork arrived late.

Two practical details we always ask buyers to confirm. First, label placement: centre of a seam, left of a seam, or loose in the pack for products like throws where a sewn label would spoil the drape. Second, wash temperature: the label must match what the product actually survives in our lab tests, not what the category usually says. A label that promises a 60 degree wash on a fabric tested at 40 is a claim you will answer for later. This is the same evidence-first thinking we describe in our quality control guide.

Brand labels, hang tags and barcodes

The brand layer is simpler but generates the most back and forth, mostly because artwork arrives in the wrong format or the positions were never agreed. Specify three things clearly:

  • Brand labels. Woven or printed, the exact artwork file, the size, and the position relative to the care label. If the brand label and care label share one seam, say so.
  • Hang tags. The artwork, the paper weight, how they attach (kimble, ribbon, safety pin) and where. If the tag carries the barcode and price, confirm which corner faces out when the product is folded, because that decides whether store staff can scan without unfolding.
  • Barcodes. The numbers come from the buyer, usually EAN-13 codes issued through GS1 membership. We print them where you tell us: on the hang tag, on a sticker on the polybag, or both. Ask your manufacturer to scan test printed codes from the first production batch. A code that fails at the till because of print contrast is a cheap problem at the factory and an expensive one at a distribution centre.
Founder note The single most useful habit I can recommend is this: approve a complete packed sample, not just a product sample. One piece, folded, labelled, tagged, bagged and photographed from every side, signed off by your team before bulk packing starts. Nearly every packaging dispute I have seen in thirty years traces back to an instruction that lived in an email thread instead of on an approved physical sample. The packed sample costs almost nothing and settles every argument in advance.

Folding, inserts and the unit presentation

How a product is folded decides how it looks on the shelf and how many pieces fit in a container, so it is worth specifying rather than leaving to habit. A duvet cover folded to show the printed face reads differently from one folded to a compact square. Bath mats are usually rolled or flat packed depending on pile height. Kitchen sets are often folded around a card so the set stays square. For each product, agree the folded dimensions, which face shows, and whether tissue, card or a board insert supports the fold.

Inserts deserve a sentence of their own. A simple printed card inside a polybag lifts the perceived value of the product more than almost any other packaging spend, and it is also where warnings, sizing and brand story text can live without adding labels to the product itself. If your range uses gift-ready presentation, ribbon ties or boxed sets, prototype them at sampling stage. Assembly time per piece affects cost, and it is better to know that before the price is fixed than after.

Polybags, belly bands and sustainable alternatives

The default unit pack for exported textiles is still the polybag, and for a reason: a container spends weeks at sea, and the bag is what stands between your product and moisture, dust and handling marks. If you use polybags, specify the thickness in microns, whether the bag is plain or printed, the closure (adhesive flap or fold), and the suffocation warning text your market requires on bags above a certain size.

More and more of our buyers are moving away from virgin plastic, and there are workable routes. Recycled content polybags are widely available and can be printed with their recycled percentage. Paper belly bands suit products with enough body to protect themselves, such as folded towelling or heavy woven cushion covers. Some programmes drop the unit bag entirely and rely on one liner per inner carton, which cuts plastic dramatically but means every piece must be handled clean at the destination. Each route is legitimate. The mistake is not choosing one and discovering at packing time that nobody specified anything. If sustainability claims appear on your packaging, make sure they match the facts of the material, the same discipline we urge for fibre claims in our certification explainer.

Inner cartons, master cartons and shipping marks

Cartons are where packaging meets logistics. The structure that works for most programmes is a unit pack inside an inner carton inside a master carton, with the inner carton holding one SKU or one assortment and the master carton built for handling. What the buyer must specify:

  • Quantities and assortments. How many pieces per inner, how many inners per master, and whether cartons are solid (one SKU) or assorted (a mix, usually matching how stores order). Assorted cartons save work downstream but must be defined exactly: which sizes, which colours, in what ratio.
  • Carton quality and weight limit. Export cartons should be specified by ply and bursting strength, and most distribution centres set a maximum gross weight per carton so a single person can lift it safely. Tell your manufacturer the limit; we will engineer the pack counts around it.
  • Shipping marks. The printed panel on each carton: buyer name or code, purchase order number, SKU, quantity, carton number in sequence, gross and net weight, dimensions and destination. Some buyers add carton barcode labels for automated receiving. Send the layout as a template and the factory will repeat it identically on every carton.

Good carton discipline shows up in your landed cost too. Carton dimensions decide how efficiently a container loads, and a badly sized master carton can waste a surprising share of the cube you are paying to ship. This sits alongside the freight decisions we walk through in our Incoterms guide. Ask your manufacturer to show the container plan before packing starts; ours comes from the same team that runs the warehouse and packing floor, so the plan reflects how the goods are actually stacked.

Market differences: EU, UK, US and beyond

A short orientation, not a legal opinion. The European Union requires fibre composition labelling under its textile regulation, using the harmonised fibre names, in the languages of the countries where the product is sold. The United Kingdom mirrors this closely with its own instrument. The United States adds registered identification numbers, specific rules on origin marking, and state level requirements for filled products. Australia and New Zealand emphasise care labelling. Middle Eastern markets often require Arabic on packaging. Japan has its own care symbol system that differs from the ISO symbols most of the world uses.

The pattern to notice is that none of this is difficult, but all of it is specific. A manufacturer who exports across these markets, as we do, will have seen your market's requirements before and can flag the obvious gaps in a spec. What we cannot do is guess your importer details or your language list. That information has to come from you, and it is best supplied at development stage, alongside the design brief, as part of the process we describe in our private label development guide.

The packaging spec sheet: what to send your manufacturer

Everything above condenses into one document per product. Ours runs to a single page and covers:

  1. Care label: content, material, size, placement, languages.
  2. Brand label and hang tag: artwork files, sizes, positions, attachment method.
  3. Barcodes: the numbers, and where each one appears.
  4. Folding: method, finished folded size, which face shows, inserts.
  5. Unit pack: polybag, belly band, box or none, with material specs and warnings.
  6. Inner carton: quantity, solid or assorted, assortment ratio.
  7. Master carton: quantity, quality, maximum gross weight.
  8. Shipping marks: the exact layout, plus any carton barcode labels.
  9. Anything market specific: importer details, law labels, language requirements.

Send that with the order and confirm it with a packed sample, and packaging stops being a risk. Leave it to the last week and it becomes the thing that holds your container at the port while everyone waits for label stock. We keep low and flexible MOQs on packaging components just as we do on the products themselves, so a spec change between seasons rarely strands material.

Frequently asked questions

What must a care label on exported home textiles include?

At minimum: the fibre composition by percentage, the care instructions (usually as wash symbols, often with text as well), and the country of origin, which for our production is made in India. Depending on the destination market you may also need the importer's name and address, a registered identification number, size information and warnings for specific products. The buyer must supply or approve the label artwork, because the legal responsibility for correct labelling sits with whoever places the product on their market.

Who supplies barcodes for private label home textiles?

The buyer does. Barcodes identify the product within your own system, so the numbers come from your side, usually EAN-13 codes issued through GS1 membership or codes allocated by your own systems team. The manufacturer prints them on stickers, hang tags or packaging exactly as supplied, and should scan test a sample of printed codes before bulk packing. Never ask a manufacturer to invent barcode numbers for you.

Do home textiles need polybags for export?

Most programmes still use them, because a polybag protects the product from moisture, dust and handling marks during a sea journey of several weeks. But they are not compulsory, and many buyers now switch to alternatives: paper belly bands, recycled content polybags marked with their recycled percentage, or one master liner per inner carton instead of a bag on every piece. What matters is deciding deliberately. Unprotected textiles in a container can arrive with damp marks or soiling that no retailer can sell.

What should a packaging spec sheet for home textiles include?

Care label content and placement, brand label and hang tag artwork with positions, barcode numbers and where each code appears, the folding method and finished folded size, the unit presentation such as polybag, belly band or box, inner carton quantity and assortment, master carton quantity, maximum carton weight, shipping mark layout, and any inserts or warnings the destination market requires. One page per product is usually enough, and it saves weeks of correspondence later.

Closing thought

Packaging is the least discussed part of a home textile programme and one of the most revealing. When a container opens at your warehouse and every carton is marked correctly, every label reads correctly and every fold presents the product the way your stores expect, that is not luck. It is the result of a one page spec, a packed sample and a factory that treats the packing floor with the same seriousness as the stitching lines.
