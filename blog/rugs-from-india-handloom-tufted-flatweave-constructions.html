Why the construction matters more than the picture

Rugs are the one category where buyers most often shop from a photograph and get a surprise in the container. Two rugs can look near identical online and be completely different objects. One is woven, one is glued. One will still look presentable after three winters in a hallway, and one will start shedding in week four. The difference is almost never the design. It is the construction underneath it.

I spend more time on this conversation than on any other category, because rug vocabulary is genuinely confusing. Handloom, hand tufted, hand knotted, flatweave, dhurrie, kilim, braided. Half of these words get used loosely in trade listings, and some are used deliberately loosely. So here is how each one is actually made in an Indian factory, what it costs you in time and money, and where each belongs in a retail range. If you want the company view of what we run, our rugs page covers the constructions we produce and the infrastructure behind them.

Handloom rugs: pile built into the weave

A handloom rug is woven on a loom, which sounds obvious but has one consequence that matters enormously. The pile and the ground are formed at the same time, as a single cloth. There is no separate step where the surface yarn is attached to a base. The rug is structurally one thing.

In practice the weaver works with a rod or gauge to lift loops of pile yarn as the ground is woven. Leave the loops intact and you get a loop pile, which is firm underfoot and hard wearing. Cut them and you get a cut pile, which is softer and catches light differently. Many of the most commercial handloom designs mix both in the same rug, so the pattern is made by texture rather than by colour, which photographs beautifully and never fades out of register.

What handloom gives you: a clean construction with no adhesive in it, good longevity, and a hand feel that customers register immediately. What it costs you: design freedom. A loom works in straight lines and repeats. Curved, painterly artwork is difficult and slow on a handloom, and pushing it there usually means paying tufted money for a loom product. Handloom rewards geometry, stripes, colour blocking, texture play and borders.

Hand tufted rugs: design freedom and a bonded back

Hand tufting starts somewhere else entirely. A primary backing cloth is stretched tight on a frame, the artwork is traced onto it, and an artisan works from the back with a hand held tufting gun, punching yarn through the cloth to build the pile. When the surface is complete, a latex coat is applied to lock the tufts, and a secondary backing, usually cotton canvas, is bonded over it. The pile is then sheared and carved to the finished height.

The great advantage is that anything you can draw, you can tuft. Curves, gradients, illustrated motifs, high and low carving within one design. Tufted rugs also go from artwork to finished sample quickly, which is why they dominate fast moving seasonal ranges. The trade off is the back. A tufted rug lives or dies on the quality of its latex and the bond of its secondary backing. Skimp there and you get shedding, a rug that curls at the edges, and in bad cases a persistent smell in a sealed container.

The practical specification points for a tufted programme are pile height, pile density, the weight of latex applied and the type of secondary backing. Ask for all four in writing. A supplier who cannot give you those numbers is probably buying the rug from someone else.

Founder note The single most useful test I can teach a rug buyer takes four seconds. Take the sample, grip a small tuft of pile between finger and thumb, and pull firmly. On a properly finished tufted rug the pile stays put. On a poor one you come away with yarn in your hand, every time, and that yarn will end up in your customer's vacuum cleaner and then in your returns data. Do it in three or four places, including near the edge, where bonding is weakest. It has saved more of my buyers' seasons than any lab report.

Flatweaves and dhurries: no pile, two good sides

A flatweave has no pile whatsoever. The design comes purely from how warp and weft interlace, which means the structure is the pattern. Indian dhurries are the classic version of this and go back centuries in the subcontinent. Kilim is the same broad idea with a different regional vocabulary.

Flatweaves earn their place in a range for reasons that have nothing to do with fashion. They are light, so freight per unit is low and a container carries a lot of them. Many are reversible, which retailers can genuinely use in copy. There is no pile to crush, so they hold their appearance in corridors and under dining chairs. And they wash better than any pile rug, which makes them the sensible choice for kitchens and entryways.

What they will not do is feel plush. A flatweave is firm underfoot, and customers who want softness will not be satisfied by one no matter how good the design is. They also need honest packing advice, because a folded flatweave can carry crease lines for weeks after unrolling. We usually recommend rolling rather than folding for the mid and large sizes, which is one of the packing specifications worth settling early, as we cover in the packaging and labelling guide.

Braided, hand knotted and shaggy constructions

Three more constructions come up often enough to be worth naming.

  • Braided rugs. Strips of fabric or thick roving are braided into a rope, then coiled and stitched together into a round, oval or rectangular rug. They read as handmade and rustic, they suit recycled fibre stories very well, and they carry a chunky texture that photographs strongly. The thing to inspect is the stitching between the coils, because that is where a braided rug comes apart.
  • Hand knotted rugs. The most labour intensive construction in the category. Individual knots are tied onto the warp by hand, row by row, and the rug is built up over weeks or months. Knot count drives both fineness and cost. This is heirloom territory, not volume retail, and it is priced accordingly. Worth knowing so that you recognise when a listing uses the term loosely.
  • Shaggy and high pile rugs. Long pile yarn, usually tufted or woven on a wide gauge, delivering the sink in feel that sells in bedrooms and living rooms. Beautiful in the right room and difficult in the wrong one. Specify the yarn carefully, since a poor shaggy yarn mats permanently after a few months.

Fibres: cotton, wool, jute, viscose and recycled blends

Construction decides how a rug is made. Fibre decides how it lives.

  • Cotton. Soft, takes colour brilliantly, washable in the smaller sizes, and priced for volume retail. The default for flatweaves, braided rugs and a great many tufted programmes. It is not the most abrasion resistant fibre, so it suits bedrooms and living spaces more than front doors.
  • Wool. The performance fibre of the category. It springs back from crushing, hides soil, resists flattening and ages well. It costs more and it takes dye differently from cotton, so wool and cotton versions of the same shade will never match exactly. Plan for that at lab dip stage rather than arguing about it later.
  • Jute and natural fibres. Strong, textural, low cost and a genuine sustainability story. Jute is coarse underfoot and does not like damp, so keep it out of bathrooms and away from wet entrances. It blends well with cotton, which softens the hand.
  • Viscose and art silk. Used for lustre. It looks like silk in a photograph and adds a sheen that no other affordable fibre gives. It is also the least forgiving fibre in the category: it marks with water, it crushes, and it is genuinely difficult to clean. Use it as an accent, never as the base of a workhorse rug, and be honest with your customer in the care copy.
  • Recycled blends. Recycled cotton and recycled polyester both work well in rugs, and rugs are actually one of the easiest categories in which to use them, because a little colour variation reads as character rather than as a fault. If you want the claim certified, decide before yarn is bought, not after. Our explainer on recycled and organic fibre options sets out what each route requires.

Sizes, shapes and range architecture

Rug sizing is more standardised than new buyers expect, and the standard sizes are standard for a reason: they fit the furniture arrangements people actually have. The sizes that carry most international ranges are roughly 60×90 cm for accents and doorways, 120×180 cm as the volume mid size, 160×230 cm for living rooms, and a runner around 60×200 cm for hallways. Larger programmes add 200×290 cm. Round rugs, usually 120 cm or 150 cm diameter, have become a reliable part of most ranges rather than a novelty.

Here is the structural advice I give almost every rug buyer. Build the range on fewer designs across more sizes, not more designs in one size. A customer choosing a rug is solving a room problem, and the size is the first filter they apply. A design that exists in only one size simply fails most of the shoppers who liked it. Three sizes of one design also share the same yarn, the same dye lots and the same set-up, which keeps minimums sensible and repeats fast. That same arithmetic runs through how minimums actually work, and it applies just as much here as it does in cushions or throws and blankets.

Backings, edges and the parts that fail first

Almost every rug complaint that reaches me is about an edge or a back, not a face. Four specifications are worth writing into the tech pack every time.

  1. Secondary backing on tufted rugs. Specify the material and confirm coverage is even to the edges. Cotton canvas is the usual choice. Check there is no dry, brittle or patchy latex, and no strong odour when the sample comes out of its bag.
  2. Edge finishing. Binding, serging or a woven selvedge, depending on construction. Whichever you choose, it has to be continuous and tight. Run a finger along the whole perimeter of the sample. You will feel a weak point long before you see it.
  3. Fringes. Decorative on most modern rugs and structural on some traditional ones. If the rug has fringes, ask how they are secured, because a fringe that pulls out takes warp ends with it.
  4. Anti-slip expectations. Rugs are not bath mats and are not usually supplied with a rubberised back, so agree early whether your market expects any slip resistance and whether you will recommend an underlay in the care copy. The bath mat conventions are different, and mixing the two up creates disappointed customers.

Weight per square metre belongs in the specification too. It is the honest summary figure for a rug, in the same way GSM is for a fabric, and it is much harder to fudge than a description of density.

What to check on the sample

When the pre-production sample arrives, give it fifteen minutes on the floor rather than two minutes on a desk.

  1. Lay it flat. It should relax within a short time and lie without waves or curled corners. Persistent waving on a tufted rug usually means uneven backing tension and will not improve in bulk.
  2. Pile test. The tuft pull described above, in several places including near the edges.
  3. Colour. Against the approved lab dip, in daylight and under store lighting. Rugs sit on floors, in low and raking light, where colour behaves differently from how it does on a wall.
  4. Rub test. A clean damp white cloth rubbed firmly across the pile. Any meaningful colour transfer needs resolving before bulk.
  5. Vacuum. Run a domestic vacuum over it for a minute. Some initial loose fibre is normal on a new pile rug. A continuing cloud is not.
  6. Measure it. Length, width and diagonals. Handmade rugs carry a small size tolerance and that is legitimate, but the tolerance should be written into your specification rather than discovered in a container.

All of this is the same discipline we apply across categories, and it works because it happens before bulk rather than after. Our quality control guide sets out how those checkpoints are staged, and the infrastructure page shows where in the process they sit.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a handloom rug and a hand tufted rug?

A handloom rug is woven on a loom, so the pile and the ground structure are created together as one cloth. A hand tufted rug is made by punching yarn through a stretched backing cloth with a tufting gun, after which a secondary backing is bonded on to lock the tufts in place. Handloom gives a cleaner, more integral construction and usually a longer life. Hand tufting gives far more design freedom, because any drawn artwork can be transferred to the frame, and it is quicker to produce. Neither is automatically better. They suit different price points and different design stories.

What is a flatweave rug and where does it work best?

A flatweave rug has no pile at all. The pattern is created by the interlacing of warp and weft, which is why flatweaves are often reversible. Indian dhurries are the best known example. They are light, they pack and ship efficiently, they suit layering over hard floors, and they work well in kitchens, hallways and seasonal ranges. Because there is no pile to crush, a flatweave keeps its appearance in traffic, though it feels firmer underfoot than a pile rug.

What rug sizes should a retail range be built around?

Most international ranges are built on a small accent size around 60 by 90 cm, a mid size around 120 by 180 cm, a large size around 160 by 230 cm, and often a runner around 60 by 200 cm. Some markets add a 200 by 290 cm room size. A commercial range usually carries one design across three sizes rather than many designs in one size, because the sizes do the selling work and the extra designs only fragment production.

What should a buyer check on a rug sample before approving bulk?

Check the pile height and density against the specification, the weight per square metre, and the colour against the approved lab dip in both daylight and store lighting. On tufted rugs, tug the pile firmly in several places to test tuft bind and check the latex and backing for even coverage and any smell. On handloom and flatweave rugs, check selvedge straightness, edge binding or fringe security, and whether the rug lies flat without waving. Finish with a rub test for colour transfer and a short vacuum test for shedding.

Closing thought

Rugs reward buyers who ask how rather than what. Once you know whether a rug is woven, tufted, flat woven or braided, almost everything else follows: what it will cost, how long it will take, how it will behave in a hallway, and whether it will still look like the photograph in two years. The design sells the first one. The construction sells the repeat.

India has been making rugs in every one of these constructions for a very long time, in workshops that sit alongside the rest of the home textile trade. That proximity is useful for a buyer, because a rug programme can be developed next to the pouffes and floor seating or the cushions that will merchandise beside it.
